# QC taste profile

Version: 0.1  
Evidence through: 2026-09-09  
Status: initial evidence-backed working profile

## What QC optimizes for

QC tends to prefer work that feels as if only this cast, this social circle, and this chain of prior events could have produced it. Character recognition and memorable specificity matter more than literary prestige or a preselected message. The ideal result is funny first, internally true to the characters, visually or narratively easy to remember, and rich enough that the audience notices a second connection after the first joke lands.

## Story decision rules

### 1. Start from characters; let theme emerge

Do not impose a solemn theme and then force the cast to demonstrate it. Begin with a character's want, contradiction, blind spot, relationship, or recurring object. Let any theme appear through the collision of those elements.

### 2. Build the character around a second self

The strongest QC characters have an immediately legible surface and a contrary inner fact:

- the soft girl who imagines herself roaring through the city on a motorcycle;
- the refined, distant student who secretly games through the night;
- the dog who is the smartest and most accomplished member of the group;
- the warm information broker who performs a fee negotiation and then talks for free.

The contradiction must change behavior, not remain a descriptive adjective. Prefer a reveal that makes earlier behavior funnier or more meaningful.

### 3. Make absurdity causal

Escalation can be extreme, dark, or ridiculous, but each step should grow from a prior character choice. A curse becomes real because Haide immediately breaks his promise; a week-long pursuit follows one careless joke; unexplained speeding tickets connect to dog hair, intelligence, and a permissive uncle. Avoid randomness that could be transferred unchanged to any cast.

### 4. Use an ensemble reaction chain

A strong premise gives several characters different jobs or reactions. Their responses should reveal personality and relationships rather than repeat the same emotion. Keep a clear main comic line, then add one or two quieter side connections instead of giving everyone equal emphasis.

### 5. Plant concrete callbacks

Prefer physical anchors—red book, baseball bat, bunny, scarf, orange bag, McDonald's, dog hair, gold Ferrari—over abstract explanation. Reuse an object later with changed meaning. The callback should reward recognition without stopping the story to explain itself.

### 6. Keep secrets perspectival

For canonical events, distinguish what happened from what each person witnessed, inferred, misunderstood, or kept secret. A character should not know information merely because the audience or author knows it. Different memories are a source of future comedy, not metadata overhead.

### 7. Deliver the joke with restraint

Prefer short dialogue, concrete action, delayed reaction, and one strategically repeated line. Leave the audience one inference to make. Do not explain the joke after it lands or turn every beat into narration about personality.

### 8. End on an altered recurring behavior

A strong ending returns to an object, habit, or earlier line and quietly changes its meaning: a supposedly hated collar is never removed; the car key is put back; the extra McDonald's appears without explanation. The ending should leave a usable continuity hook rather than close with a moral.

### 9. Protect funny over important

QC Kindergarten is entertainment. Depth is welcome when it emerges from character and social consequences, but do not inflate a small comic premise into a universal statement. “Cute, strange, specific, and funny” is a valid endpoint.

### 10. Let the represented characters push back

Meta-fiction and negotiated representation fit the project when characters can challenge the author, reject their own assigned traits, or turn production history into an in-world joke. Their pushback should itself remain consistent with their personalities.

## Visual decision rules

### 11. Identity must read before detail

Separate recurring characters through silhouette, hair shape, theme color, signature prop, and posture. Do not accept near-duplicate hairstyles or colors merely because each image is individually attractive.

### 12. Compose a captured incident, not a lineup

For ensemble art, use one dominant action, a readable reaction chain, and quieter peripheral stories. QC responds to hierarchy, scale, contact, sightlines, and meaningful negative space. Avoid evenly spaced figures with equal poses and equal visual weight.

### 13. Props perform narrative work

A prop should expose character, cause action, carry a callback, or create a relationship line. Decorative accessories may support identity, but they should not compete with the story-bearing object.

### 14. Genre transformation may be bold, identity anchors stay intact

Alternate costumes and sharp tonal shifts—cute felt figures restaged as biker-gang witches, for example—are welcome. Preserve each character's palette relationship, signature object, emotional contradiction, and recognizable silhouette so the transformation reads as role-play rather than replacement.

### 15. Main and temporary people occupy different visual classes

Long-term characters have expressive, readable eyes. Temporary or background people in this project have no drawn eyes; the eye region is replaced by deliberate shadow. They share the felted-toy world but use simpler shapes, fewer signature accessories, and lower contrast.

### 16. Revise locally once the composition is approved

When QC names a specific defect, preserve unrelated approved elements. Correct contact, anatomy, scale, shadow, object orientation, or empty-space balance precisely instead of regenerating the entire creative concept without need.

## Negative boundaries

- Do not reduce a character to MBTI or a single trope.
- Do not use interchangeable banter that could belong to any cast.
- Do not add spectacle that lacks a character cause.
- Do not make “cute” mean harmless, sentimental, or conflict-free.
- Do not make every character equally loud or equally central.
- Do not expose a secret inside a limited character viewpoint.
- Do not recycle an existing punchline merely to appear consistent.
- Do not over-explain emotional subtext, symbolism, or the final joke.
- Do not confuse production workflow preferences with creative taste.

## Applying the profile without flattening it

Use the smallest relevant subset of rules. A quiet scene may need contradiction, viewpoint, and a restrained callback but no large escalation. A poster may need silhouette and hierarchy but no narrative ending. Novelty is successful when the result follows the same selection logic without resembling an existing episode beat-for-beat.
